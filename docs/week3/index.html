What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

I split it into two sites, r1 and r2 in one and r3 and r4 in the other, and joined the four routers in a ring so there is always a second path. Each site's two LANs are next to each other (10.8.0.0/24 and 10.8.1.0/24, then 10.8.2.0/24 and 10.8.3.0/24), so one /23 covers a whole site. The links are /30s named after their routers, like 10.8.14.0/30 for r1–r4.

Hop 3 to h3 in traces.txt is * * * because r3 replies from 10.8.23.2 and r1 has no route back to that link, so it drops it. h3 still answers.

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

r1's 10.8.2.0/23 via r4 covers h3's and h4's LANs, since a /23 is exactly 10.8.2.0 to 10.8.3.255. summary.txt shows both going via 10.8.14.2. If I added another network in that range on a different router, r1 would still send it to r4 unless I gave it a more specific route.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

I took down r1–r4. In failure.txt the trace to h3 gets nothing and r1 says Network is unreachable, because the /23 route went away with the link. The path through r2 still worked, but static routes cannot find it by themselves. OSPF, or a backup route via r2 with a higher metric, would have recovered.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

Me. I worked out all 8 routes in network.yaml, check_paths() checked them and the script pushed them. With 40 routers that is way too many routes to do by hand and fix after every failure, so you would want a routing protocol or a controller doing it.
